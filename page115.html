Most engineering teams capable of building an internal AI knowledge system think they should. But six months later, it's a platform team's full-time job. Building an in-house AI context layer costs more money and energy than you think.

Most engineering teams capable of building an internal AI knowledge system think they should. Stand up a vector database, wire in a few connectors, point a RAG pipeline at your docs—easy, right?

Six months later, it's a platform team's full-time job.

That's not because anyone underestimated the database work, but because "vector database" and "governed knowledge pipeline" are two different classes of problem. One is a weekend project, but the other is a trust, compliance, and continuous-maintenance challenge that compounds with every new source, every stale doc, and every agent you plug in.

The trap lies in underestimating what it costs to keep that pipeline up and running. None of these are one-and-done costs; they compound with the sophistication of your AI stack.

Writing a connector isn't the hard part. That’s keeping it current as vendor APIs evolve. And bear in mind that even a perfect connector only gets you a copy of the source. After all, agents can't skim content the way humans can: if you feed one a raw Slack thread, it will often retrieve the right document but the wrong section.

That's why capture needs to produce something more durable than a stored copy: a knowledge node, or a living record that carries the substance of what's known, where it came from, who owns it, and how much to trust it. If the source tool disappears tomorrow, the knowledge stays put.

This is the layer most build-it-yourself pipelines skip, but it's the one that matters most. Treating all captured content as equally trustworthy is the single biggest mistake in enterprise AI context layers. Some knowledge is authoritative; some is a reversed decision nobody labeled accordingly. Without a way to tell the difference, agents will surface both with equal confidence.

Validation works in two modes: implicit trust, which is automatic, and explicit trust, which is a human-in-the-loop fallback for when the stakes are too high for guesswork.

Every knowledge node carries a trust score built from multiple signals: factors like authorship, usage, recency, source, and whether it conflicts with other knowledge. Most content earns trust automatically, in the form of a merged PR from a principal engineer or a finalized policy with a named owner.

Trust also decays, however. A node that was accurate six months ago may not be today, so a node that falls below a confidence threshold surfaces a lightweight one-tap revalidation request to its owner.

The health metric that matters here is, How many times did we interrupt a human today? Every interruption is a signal that the automatic layer isn't working well enough yet.

Not all knowledge merits the same audience. A decision made by one engineer about their own service, for instance, shouldn't carry the same weight—or reach the same people—as a company-wide architectural commitment. We solve the problem of organization with scopes solves this by giving knowledge context, defined by what information is in scope and who can work within that scope. This organization is auto-populated rather than manually filed, so knowledge naturally settles into the right spaces instead of pooling into one undifferentiated room that everyone has to wade through.

Knowledge starts personal and private by default: the context a developer keeps re-explaining to their AI tools every session about who should and shouldn’t have access is instead inherited through scopes. Scopes organize knowledge into the relevant context and apply those inherited permissions from ingestion when retrieving and composing an answer. Agents inherit the same permission boundary as a human operating in that same context, so a developer's personal knowledge never leaks to their team without their permission, and a team's knowledge never leaks to the wider org unless someone explicitly chooses to share it.

Governance for AI has to work as an automated system that plugs into the tools enterprise IT and security teams already use. Buyers signing off on a company-wide AI rollout have their own requirements: they need to set permissions in bulk rather than one person at a time, they need proof of who accessed what to feed into their existing security monitoring, and they need visibility into how the system is being used without having to log into yet another dashboard.

Govern has to satisfy both audiences at once: the person browsing a settings page and the automated systems checking in behind the scenes.

As agents take on more consequential work like answering support queries or informing product decisions, the ability to trace any AI output back to a specific piece of validated content, approved by a named reviewer on a specific date, becomes a compliance requirement instead of a nice-to-have.

Validated, organized, governed knowledge that nobody encounters might as well be locked in a filing cabinet. Delivery has two modes: explicit, where a user asks and gets the best available answer with a trust score and provenance trail attached, and proactive, where the system surfaces relevant knowledge before anyone asks (for instance, an engineer opens a new service and the system already knows a similar team hit this exact bottleneck six months ago).

The Model Context Protocol (MCP) makes this composable instead of bespoke. You have one standardized interface instead of every agent building its own retrieval integration, so you’re constantly querying the current, validated state of the graph rather than a stale snapshot.

The answer to that question hinges on what your engineering org is actually for. If knowledge infrastructure is your competitive advantage, then owning that pipeline is a legitimate play. But for most product companies and developer platforms, the pipeline just needs to work reliably without consuming engineering cycles that should go toward what customers actually pay for.

Connector maintenance, trust-model tuning, SME workflows that scale with headcount, governance requirements that show up the moment an auditor asks for a trail…as we mentioned, none of these are one-time expenditures, and they compound as your AI stack gets more sophisticated. The build vs. buy decision for knowledge infrastructure boils down to a question of organizational focus. Building a vector database is easily achievable. Building a governed, trusted knowledge pipeline? Not so much.

We wanted that logic to live in one place and go wherever your handler goes. So we built Supabase Middleware. You define each piece once, compose them in order, and use them on any fetch runtime or inside a framework.

Here's what that looks like in a handler. The setup from @supabase/server and the logic you add on top become entries in the same list.

A request goes through the entries in order. withCors answers the preflight and adds CORS headers to responses. withRequiredClaims verifies the caller's JWT; if it's invalid, the chain stops there and the caller gets a 401. withSupabaseClient adds ctx.supabase, a client scoped to that caller. Then your handler runs, and all it contains is the part that's yours.

Each entry does one job and doesn't know about the handler it sits in. It adds one typed key to a shared ctx, and your handler sees everything added before it, with the right types. An entry can also return a Response and stop the chain, or read and change the response on its way out. That's why you can move an entry to another handler without rewriting it.

@supabase/middleware is the engine behind the pipeline above, and withSupabase in @supabase/server runs on it. @supabase/server also ships the middleware below as standalone entries. Two of them, withSupabaseClient and withSupabaseAdminClient, are the same pieces withSupabase is built from. The engine adds withCors and withFeatureFlag.

Most of these aren't new capabilities. ctx.supabase, for example, has been documented as RLS-scoped since May. What's new is the engine. Every piece is an entry in the same list, so you can add one, drop one, or write your own, and the same list goes wherever your handler goes.

You can pick the runtime or framework that fits the job and change it later without rewriting your per-request logic. This section shows how that works on other runtimes and inside a framework.

Moving to another runtime doesn't mean rewriting your logic. That matters when your code doesn't all run in one place. Say some of your functions run on Supabase Edge Functions and others on Vercel Functions or Cloudflare Workers, and the auth and CORS rules should match on both. You write those rules once, and each runtime runs them.

This works because the pipeline in the example above is a standard fetch handler: a function that takes a Request and returns a Response. Supabase Edge Functions, Vercel Functions, Cloudflare Workers, Deno, Bun, and Node 22 or newer can all run one, and the engine is plain Web Fetch.

Using the engine doesn't tie you to Supabase. It needs no database, no Supabase project, and no account, and it's MIT-licensed. So if you leave Supabase, the engine and everything you wrote with it go with you.

Note that one middleware doesn't run everywhere. withPostgresClient and withPostgresAdminClient don't run on Cloudflare Workers, because pg needs a TCP socket. The limit comes from pg. The engine itself runs wherever fetch does. On Workers, use withSupabaseClient and ctx.supabase instead.

Adopting a framework doesn't require rewriting your logic either. For example, say your Edge Functions are plain fetch handlers, your API runs on Hono, and the same auth and CORS rules should apply to both.

Each framework has its own middleware format and its own context object, so middleware written for one doesn't work in another. #363. Faster config, migrations and functions on branches

📅 Oct 15, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Supabase branches are their own projects, so three things have to land on one before you can use it: settings, schema, and edge functions. We run all three mostly in parallel when you create the branch.

You create a preview branch to test a schema change. About 20 seconds later (the median), the branch is ready, with your tables, settings, and Edge Functions in place. When a branch takes longer than expected, or a step fails, you need to know what ran in between.

This post walks through how a branching workflow runs in Supabase, what gets copied from your main project, where your migrations and functions come from, what order the steps run in, and what you see when something fails.

Every branch belongs to a main project. How the branch gets its inputs depends on one primary question: is the branch tied to a git branch?

GitHub-connected branches are tied to a git branch, so they read config.toml, migrations, seed files, and functions from your repo. Gitless branches have no git branch. You create them from the dashboard or the CLI, and they take their schema and functions from your main project.

You can learn more about both setups in the Branching guide.

We give each branch its own project with default settings. Then the following three parts run, mostly in parallel.

You can follow progress in the branch's workflow run. A step is RUNNING while it works, EXITED when it succeeds, PAUSED when it finishes with a warning or is skipped on purpose, and DEAD when it fails.

Your branch starts with the same settings as your main project. We copy your Postgres settings, your connection pooler (Supavisor) settings, and your Auth, Data API, Realtime, and Storage config. SSL enforcement comes across too, along with your network restrictions if your main project has them. Auth secrets don't carry over. We also turn on Database Webhooks for the branch.

Some Postgres settings only take effect after a restart, and restarts are slow. So the update_config job writes all the Postgres settings to disk first, without restarting. Most new branches then turn on backups with the walg_enable job, which restarts Postgres once. That one restart picks up the new settings too.

If your main project enforces SSL, we apply SSL enforcement to the branch before walg_enable takes the first backup. SSL enforcement restarts Postgres, and a restart during a backup would leave a backup you can't restore from.

Next, the infra_update job applies the Auth, Data API, and Realtime config and restarts Auth and the Data API so they pick it up. When that job finishes, the configure step goes EXITED. If update_config or infra_update fails, configure goes DEAD.

Two more jobs run alongside these: one applies your network restrictions to the branch when your main project has them, and one registers the branch with the connection pooler. Both, along with SSL enforcement, retry on their own. They don't hold up configure or fail the branch.

A GitHub-connected branch then runs its own workflow, and its configure step applies the config.toml in your repo. Your repo's settings land on top of the copied ones, so the repo wins. We wait for the copied config to land before starting this workflow, so the two never race.

The configure step reads the branch's current config, then updates the Postgres, Data API, Auth, and Storage sections that differ from config.toml. It retries on failure, skipping sections that already applied, and ends with a health check so the restarted services are back up.

If your repo has no config.toml, the step turns on Database Webhooks and stops there. To give a long-lived persistent branch its own settings, add a [remotes] block. You can read more about branch configuration with remotes.

Migrations give your branch the same schema as your main project, or the schema your pull request describes.

A GitHub-connected branch runs this workflow on every push:

Each step waits for the one before it. On the first push, migrations start only after the copied settings are in place.

A gitless branch runs a shorter workflow, pull < migrate. We start it after the last Postgres restart from configuration, since a restart in the middle of a migration would drop its connection.

The pull step gathers the migration files to apply. The source depends on the branch. On a GitHub-connected branch, the clone step checks out your git branch, so the files in supabase/migrations are already there. A gitless branch reads supabase_migrations.schema_migrations on your main project. Each row holds a version, a name, and the statements that ran, and pull turns each row back into a migration file. It also reads the branch's own history, so migrations already applied on the branch stay in the set.

If your main project has no migration history, pull dumps its schema and uses the dump as the first migration. It also records that migration in your main project's history table, which means later branches and supabase db pull start from it.

The migrate step connects to the branch database and compares your migration files with the branch's supabase_migrations.schema_migrations table. Any file without a matching row is pending. Before running anything, migrate saves the Vault secrets from config.toml to your branch. We suggest encrypting the secrets with dotenvx if you plan to commit them in your repo. You can turn migrations off with [db.migrations].

Each file is split into statements. A ; ends a statement only outside string literals, quoted names, dollar-quoted or BEGIN ATOMIC function bodies, comments, and parentheses. The migration file is expected to contain only valid SQL. We don't support psql meta-commands like COPY FROM STDIN.

The splitter is written in native Go and aims to be 100% compatible with psql's own lexer: a file that psql splits one way should split the same way here. Our implementation is validated against PG 16 using a corpus that includes 121 hand-written edge cases, 199 SQL files from the Postgres regression tests, and about 8,000 random fuzz inputs with automatic shrinking.

Each file then runs in pipeline mode. We send every statement in the file, plus the row for the history table, to Postgres in one round trip. The batch runs as one transaction. A file either applies in full and gets its history row, or rolls back and leaves no trace.

When a migration fails, the error shows which statement failed and points a caret at the position. A migration file older than the latest applied one still runs, with a warning. A version in the history table with no matching file fails the step.

After migrate, the seed step creates the Storage buckets declared in config.toml and uploads their files. If seeding is on in [db.seed], it also runs your seed files. Read about seeding branches.

Your branch gets the same Edge Functions as your main project, or the ones in your repo.

The copy_functions job copies every active function from your main project onto the branch. The copy runs in parallel with configuration, since functions don't live in the branch's Postgres database. The branch isn't reported ready until the copy finishes. The deploy step goes EXITED on success and DEAD on failure.

The copy is safe to retry. A function redeployed on your main project during the copy is never registered on the branch with a missing bundle.

A GitHub-connected branch runs deploy as the last step of its workflow. If your repo has a supabase/functions directory, deploy deploys the functions declared in config.toml under [functions.]. If your repo has no functions directory, deploy copies the functions deployed on your main project, and config.toml can still turn a function off or change its JWT check. Function secrets in config.toml are saved first. If your repo has functions but none are declared in config.toml, the step warns you and deploys nothing.

Only changed functions are uploaded again. For each function, deploy builds the bundle and compresses it with Brotli, then computes a SHA-256 checksum of the compressed bundle. It compares the checksum and the JWT setting with the values stored for the function already on the branch. If both match, it skips the function. Otherwise it uploads the new bundle.

Every deploy uses the same compression settings, so an unchanged function always produces the same checksum. A function copied from your main project reuses the main project's compressed bundle when one exists, so the same check applies.

Most failures resolve on their own, since setup jobs retry before giving up. When a step does fail, a DEAD step stops the steps that depend on it. If migrate fails, the seed and deploy steps don't run. A PAUSED step finished with a warning, and the steps after it still run. A failed health check is a warning, not a failure. If a Postgres restart during setup fails for good, a gitless branch's migrate step stays CREATED, since no migration ran.

To see what happened, open your project dashboard, go to Manage Branches, select the branch, and open its logs. The troubleshooting guide covers common issues.

Connect your GitHub repo and open a pull request to see the full workflow run. Start with the GitHub integration guide, or create your first branch from the dashboard with branching via the dashboard.

Our new repo star history API now provides trends without returning individual identities or requiring unbounded database work. These are the engineering tradeoffs we made.

Maintainers look at star history to answer a practical question: did a launch, release, or event change a project’s trajectory? To reconstruct that curve from the GitHub API, tools had to request pages of stargazer records (each person who currently stars the repository and when they starred it), keep the timestamps, and throw the identities away.

Over time, that design created problems at both ends. Identities were available for bulk collection, so bad actors started scraping public stargazer lists for spam and phishing campaigns. For highly starred repositories, the endpoint could also make the database process many thousands of entries to return a single page.

The fix seemed clear: return counts instead of people. But returning less data didn’t actually make the query any cheaper.

Now maintainers have a direct way to get the curve: we built an endpoint that returns up to 30 weeks of daily star counts in a single request, with no identities attached. Keeping that cheap to serve meant reducing how much data the database processed, not just how much it returned, and the endpoint does that by filtering stars by time before it counts anything.

We first addressed the immediate risks to developers and to our database by restricting stargazer and watcher lists to repository administrators and collaborators. After the access change, callers without the required permission could receive an empty response or a 403 Forbidden response.

That change also cut off tools that had used the old response to calculate star history, often without displaying or otherwise using the usernames it returned. So we talked with maintainers and tool builders to understand what they still needed. They used star history as an approximate signal of interest: to compare activity before and after a release, or to see whether an announcement reached new developers. What they cared about was the curve.

The old API response had made identity look like part of that contract because every timestamp arrived attached to a user. In practice, tools such as Star History needed counts and dates, not usernames or other user data. Once identity was no longer part of the requirement, we could design an aggregate endpoint for the actual job: retrieving changes in star counts over time at a useful resolution.

When the API change disrupted Star History, users rallied around the project. GitHub’s Community team listened and connected us with product engineering, who dug into our use case and worked alongside us to design a purpose-built endpoint. A difficult situation became a constructive collaboration between GitHub, Star History, and the broader community—ultimately leading to a better solution.

Once we knew what maintainers needed, the next step was understanding why the original design couldn’t provide it cheaply. The original GET /repos/{owner}/{repo}/stargazers endpoint was built to answer a simple question: who starred this repository? It returned a paginated list of people, and if the request specified a custom media type, each entry also included when the star was created.

The star model was built for the same job. It records the current set of people who have starred a repository, which keeps showing a star count or a list of stargazers simple. It was not designed as a historical event log: when someone removes a star, that record is removed. When tools began using the list as a time series, reconstructing a growth curve meant retrieving individual identities and processing every current star. Because each page held at most 100 people, the work grew with the repository’s popularity: building the full curve for react/react, with about 251,000 stars, took about 2,511 requests, so tools often ran into rate limits or sampled pages instead of reading them all.

Serving a page required the database to query the repository’s current stars. Due to a bug in the pagination implementation, every request also counted all of those stars to work out how many pages existed, even though the caller received only one page of up to 100 people. On a highly starred repository, that meant counting many thousands of entries on every request. In the week before we restricted access, nearly 37,000 of these count queries ran slowly enough to be logged, averaging nearly 3 seconds each, and database safeguards killed more than 1,000 of them, which contributed to request timeouts.

The chart below shows what all that counting cost. After we restricted access, the database served about 60% fewer stargazer requests, and the total time it spent on the count query fell by about 80%.

Removing identities handled the privacy requirement. The harder part was keeping each request from scanning the repository’s entire current star set.

Our first spike kept one record per star and returned the starred_at value without the stargazer’s identity for callers who lacked collaborator access. At first, this looked like the quickest path forward, because it directly served the request we had heard from tool builders. It also closed the identity-harvesting path with a relatively small change to the existing serialization and authorization flow.

This approach addressed identity exposure and would have allowed existing tools to keep working. But API consumers would still have to paginate through and process potentially many thousands of individual records to build an aggregate history. It also tied the new use case to an endpoint and pagination model designed for listing people rather than retrieving trends.

Aggregation seemed like the obvious next step. Instead of returning one row per star, the query could group stars into daily or weekly buckets, sort those buckets, and paginate the much smaller result.

The problem was where pagination happened. In a straightforward SQL query, the database first finds all stars for the repository, groups them into buckets, and sorts those buckets. Only then does it apply LIMIT, which controls how many buckets are returned, and OFFSET, which determines where the page begins:

For a popular repository, an anonymous request could still scan and aggregate many thousands of index entries to return a handful of buckets. Returning fewer buckets would shrink the JSON response, but it would not reduce the expensive grouping and sorting the database had already performed. For react/react, reading the full history that way would have meant examining roughly 6 million rows, because every one of the 24 requests would have reprocessed all of its stars. To reduce the query cost, we had to narrow the rows before aggregation began.

The design we shipped paginates star history by calendar week. For each request, it translates page and per_page into an indexed created_at range, filters the stars to those weeks, and then groups the matching records into daily counts. A response includes up to 30 weeks, and increasing page moves backward through the repository’s history.

With the date range applied first, each request processes the weeks being returned instead of every current star in the repository. For tools, that means one request now covers up to 30 weeks of history, no matter how many stars the repository has. For react/react, that’s about 24 requests instead of about 2,511, and the database examines each star about once (roughly 251,000 rows) across the full history. This fits the primary use case because maintainers usually inspect growth across weeks or around a known release, launch, or event. Week-based ranges also align with other repository activity APIs: the response follows the same shape as the weekly commit activity endpoint, GET /repos/{owner}/{repo}/stats/commit_activity.

The time-range design limits each request to a defined period, but it does not limit the number of stars within that range. A week with an unusually large number of stars can therefore still produce a slow or expensive query. The normal request path no longer queries every current star, but exceptionally dense weeks remain a scaling limitation.

Comparing the performance of the old and new API paths also surfaced other places where GitHub was counting stars on demand. We found the same pattern on the repository stargazers page on GitHub.com and updated it to use the repository’s cached star count. Slow queries from that page, which had numbered in the hundreds each week, dropped to zero. We recently shipped a similar fix to other stargazer API endpoints.

Hyperdrive is a distributed database connection pooler that connects Cloudflare Workers to SQL databases. Hyperdrive holds warm SQL connections open on our global network and shares these connections across many Worker requests. In addition to connection pooling, Hyperdrive can also cache common queries in our global CDN cache, reducing latency and load on the origin database.

Establishing a connection to a SQL database is expensive, taking a minimum of five network round trips. On a serverless computeÂ platform like Workers, establishing a connection for each request would be impractical, both because of the amount of latency added to each request, but also because a large number of open connections can incur high per-connection resource costs. While MySQL fares better here than Postgres since MySQL doesnât use an entire OS process per connection, a typical deployment can still run into resource issues with a large number of connections.

For more details, see our blog post introducing Hyperdrive.

Some of the issues we ran into were small and easy to fix once reported. One of the first problems reported in the beta was that emojis written to the database were not being correctly returned. This was a bug related to character set encoding. Hyperdrive negotiates the encoding for the connection in the startup handshake, and we negotiate utf8Â as the encoding. As it turns out, utf8Â is a deprecated aliasÂ for utf8mb3Â which is notÂ all of UTF-8: it only supports 1â3 bytes per Unicode code point, while full UTF-8 has a max of 4 bytes per code point. To get all of UTF-8, we needed to change our handshake to use utf8mb4Â , which fixed the emoji issue.

Another encoding-related bug occurred when we added connection cleanup via COM_RESET_CONNECTION. This is a lightweight MySQL command to clear any session state in a given connection, which is exactly what Hyperdrive needs to ensure that no session-level state leaks between checkouts. But adding this connection cleanup step caused encoding issues once again! It turns out that COM_RESET_CONNECTION does not reset the connection state to the character set encoding we negotiated at connection startup; it instead resets the connection to the server default. This means that after negotiating utf8mb4 on a MySQL server instance that defaults to latin1, once we run COM_RESET_CONNECTION, our connection now has an encoding of latin1. The fix is to always run a command setting the encoding to utf8mb4 after a connection reset.

To understand Hyperdrive's caching, we first need to talk about Hyperdrive's topology and how it interacts with the CDN cache. Every Hyperdrive instance has a split architecture with two nodes: a Client and an Endpoint. The Client node runs on the same machine as the userâs Worker, handles local caching (if enabled), andÂ forwards queries to the Endpoint if needed.Â The Endpoint node holds the connection pool and is placed in the data center closest to the origin database. Each node uses the Cloudflare CDN cache local to the data center it is running in. Importantly, this means that if the two nodes are running in the same data center, they will share a cache.

Sharing the cache between Client and Endpoint is generally good for performance. We use a hash of the contents of the queryâs wire messages with some normalization and store the result bytes in the cache. If a Hyperdrive Client running in the same data center as its Endpoint receives a request, and that Endpoint has recently cached the results of the same query, the Client can serve the response directly from the shared data center cache. To work correctly, this scheme requires that the same bytes are cached for the same response, whether stored by the Client or the Endpoint.

Since we couldnât get a direct trace of the issue, we decided to spend time looking at the MySQL client / server protocol. Perhaps we had an assumption about Postgresâ protocol built into our MySQL handling that was causing these issues.

The protocol describes how a MySQL client communicates with a MySQL server. This can be somewhat confusing in the case of database connection pooling since we have two components acting like clients and two acting like servers. The first client is the Worker using a MySQL client library, and it connects to the first component acting like a server, the Hyperdrive Client. The Hyperdrive Client connects to the Hyperdrive Endpoint which acts as a MySQL client connecting to the actual MySQL origin server.

This protocol starts with the Connection PhaseÂ where the MySQL client and the MySQL server agree upon a common authentication method and capabilities. Once a connection is established, the client and server begin the Command Phase, where the client sends commands (e.g. SQL statements) to be executed with the server responding and waiting for the next command.

Capabilities are exchanged during the Connection Phase to support protocol evolution while remaining backwards compatible. Each capability is a feature, like supporting compression during the Command Phase. The clients and servers must only use features which the other supports, otherwise a protocol desync can occur, leading to unexpected behavior.

This capability negotiation can be tricky for Hyperdrive, since Hyperdrive acts as a client to the origin database, and acts as a server to the Worker. To ensure that Hyperdrive can parse the messages from the origin MySQL server, Hyperdrive has a fixed set of capability flags which are used to communicate to the origin database. Since it is possible for Hyperdriveâs set of capabilities to be different from the Worker clientâs set of capabilities, Hyperdrive needs to translate all origin responses encoded by its capabilities to the capabilities that the Worker expects.

While trying to debug this issue, we found that a popular MySQL client library for Node.js (mysql2) does not currently supportÂ the CLIENT_DEPRECATE_EOFÂ capability, which changes how result sets are terminated. Without this capability, the server sends an EOF packet between the column definitions and rows, then another EOF packet at the end of the result set. When CLIENT_DEPRECATE_EOF is negotiated, the metadata EOF packet is omitted and the final EOF packet is replaced with an OK packet. MySQL server 5.7.5 and later supports this capability, but the newer OK-packet behavior is only used when the client negotiates it.

While Hyperdrive doesÂ support the CLIENT_DEPRECATE_EOF capability, itâs possible that the Workerâs MySQL driver does not. To handle this, Hyperdrive adjusts its behavior to support the capabilities that the Worker client driver expects. This means rewriting result sets to include EOFs and to translate all OK packets to EOFs when required.Â

We finally came across the last missing piece: a faulty assumption in our Hyperdrive Client caching code. We baked in an assumption when implementing the Client caching for Postgres: that we can cache exactly the bytes we wrote back to the Worker. While this was a reasonable assumption to make with Postgresâ wire protocol, it no longer holds due to the mismatch in MySQL capabilities. If a Client node is caching exactly what it writes back to the Worker, these bytes are in a different format than what an Endpoint node would be expecting. Thatâs because the Endpoint node is always working with Hyperdriveâs capabilities, while the Client node writes back bytes that are translated to match the Workerâs capabilities.

#143. Why Is My Kubernetes Job Still Pending? Tracing Kueue Admission with Headlamp

You submit a Kubernetes Job and wait for its Pods to appear. A minute passes. Then another. The Job is still suspended, there are no application logs, and there may not even be a Pod to inspect.

In a cluster managed by Kueue, this can be completely normal. Kueue may still be deciding whether the Job can use the requested resources. The useful question is not only “Why is the Pod pending?” It is first “Has Kueue admitted the Workload?”

The Kueue plugin for Headlamp brings that investigation into the Headlamp UI. Instead of reconstructing the admission path from several commands and large YAML objects, you can begin with the Workload, read its condition message, and follow links through the LocalQueue, ClusterQueue, ResourceFlavor, and Cohort.

This article follows that Headlamp-first path. The command line appears only in a short optional section for reproducing the example.

You need a Kubernetes cluster with Kueue installed, a Headlamp installation, and the 0.1.0-alpha Kueue plugin. Your Kubernetes identity must be allowed to read the Kueue resources used in this article.

The alpha release provides read-only views for Kueue v1beta2 Cohorts, ClusterQueues, LocalQueues, ResourceFlavors, and Workloads. It does not add its own create, edit, stop, resume, or delete workflows. Headlamp still provides its standard YAML and resource actions where your permissions allow them.

Kueue and the Kubernetes scheduler answer different questions.

Kueue admission: Should this Workload start now, considering queue policy, quota, ResourceFlavors, admission checks, and other waiting Workloads?

Pod scheduling: After the Workload is admitted and the Job resumes, which nodes can run its Pods?

That boundary changes the investigation. If the Workload is not admitted, stay in the Kueue section of Headlamp. If it is admitted but its Pods are still Pending, move to Headlamp's normal Job and Pod views and inspect scheduler events.

The quickest investigation starts with the Workload, not with a shell.

In Headlamp, open Kueue → Workloads. Select the namespace that contains the Job, then find the Workload created for it.

Kueue creates a Workload to represent the resource request and admission state of a Job. In the alpha plugin, owner references are shown as Kind/name text but are not yet clickable. The Workload name normally contains the Job name, so the namespace filter and table search are the simplest ways to locate it.

Before opening the detail page, the list already gives you a useful first scan:

a readable current status such as Admitted, Finished, or Inadmissible

If there is no matching Workload, check three things before going further: the selected namespace, whether the Job was submitted to a LocalQueue, and whether your Kubernetes account can list Workloads. The plugin performs permission checks and displays an access message when the current identity cannot read a resource.

Open the Workload. Start with its current status and conditions, not with the full YAML.

For admission troubleshooting, the most important condition types are QuotaReserved and Admitted.

Admitted=True means Kueue has admitted the Workload. The Job can resume, so any remaining Pending Pods belong to the scheduling branch of the investigation.

QuotaReserved=False with a reason such as Pending means Kueue has not reserved quota. The condition message explains what prevented the reservation.

An Inadmissible status means the Workload currently cannot fit the available queue configuration. A missing covered resource, unsuitable ResourceFlavor, stopped queue, or admission check can all lead you here.

For example, a condition message may report that Kueue could not assign a ResourceFlavor because the ClusterQueue has insufficient CPU quota. That message is the first real answer to “Why is my Job still pending?” The rest of the Headlamp journey explains the configuration behind it.

Also check the Events section. Conditions describe the current state; events help show what recently changed or what Kueue attempted.

Stay on the Workload detail page and inspect the PodSets and resource requests. The plugin shows the requested Pod count, CPU, memory, and other resources that Kueue evaluates together.

This matters because a Job with parallelism: 3 and a one-CPU request per Pod asks Kueue for three CPUs in total. Looking at only one Pod's template would underestimate the Workload.

If quota has already been reserved, the same page also shows the assigned ClusterQueue, ResourceFlavors, and admitted resource usage. If those admission assignments are absent, the Workload has not reached that point yet.

The next question is where that request entered Kueue.

Use the LocalQueue link on the Workload detail page. A LocalQueue is the namespace-level entry point used by the Job. It connects namespaced Workloads to a cluster-scoped ClusterQueue.

pending, reserving, and admitted Workload counts

This page answers two practical questions: Did the Job enter the queue you expected, and is it the only Workload waiting there?

If the LocalQueue is inactive or stopped, that may already explain the wait. If it is active, follow the linked ClusterQueue to inspect the capacity behind it.

A ClusterQueue represents the cluster-level quota and admission policy available to one or more LocalQueues.

On its Headlamp detail page, compare the Workload request with:

pending, reserving, and admitted Workload counts

preemption, fair-sharing, flavor-fungibility, and admission-check settings

For the example in this article, the ClusterQueue provides a nominal quota of two CPUs and two Pod slots. The Workload asks for three of each. The condition message and the ClusterQueue page now tell the same story: the Workload does not fit, so Kueue leaves the Job suspended.

This is an admission-capacity problem. It is not evidence that kube-scheduler failed to find a node.

Quota is not always a single pool. A ResourceFlavor can represent a class of compute through node labels, taints, tolerations, or topology settings. Use the ResourceFlavor link from the ClusterQueue when the condition mentions flavor assignment or when the Workload requires a particular hardware class, such as GPU nodes.

If the ClusterQueue belongs to a Cohort, follow that link too. Cohorts let ClusterQueues share unused quota. The Headlamp page shows parent and child Cohorts, member ClusterQueues, referenced ResourceFlavors, resource groups, borrowing and lending limits, and fair-sharing information.

These pages help answer a more subtle question: Is the Workload blocked because the queue has no quota of its own, because it cannot borrow enough, or because no suitable flavor can be assigned?

For the sample pending Job, the UI gives us a complete explanation:

Its QuotaReserved condition is false and the message points to insufficient quota.

The Workload requests three CPUs and three Pod slots.

Its LocalQueue is active and points to the expected ClusterQueue.

The ClusterQueue provides only two CPUs and two Pod slots.

Kueue therefore keeps the Job suspended until quota becomes available or the queue configuration changes.

The important part is not that Headlamp invents a new status. It reads the same Kubernetes API objects as other clients, then places the related resources and human-readable values in one connected investigation.

Suppose the Workload detail page shows Admitted=True, but the application is still not running. Kueue has completed its part of the decision. Continue in Headlamp's standard Kubernetes views:

Open Workloads → Jobs and inspect the Job state and events.

Open Workloads → Pods and filter by the Job name or namespace.

Select a Pending Pod and read its Events section.

At this stage, look for scheduler concerns such as insufficient node capacity, node affinity, taints and tolerations, topology constraints, persistent-volume binding, or image-pull failures.

This handoff is the most useful troubleshooting rule in the article:

Not admitted: investigate Kueue resources in Headlamp.

Admitted, but Pods are Pending: investigate Job, Pod, node, and scheduler information in Headlamp.

When the next Job does not start, use this short path:

Open Kueue → Workloads and select the correct namespace.

Find the Workload associated with the Job.

Check the readable status, Admitted, and QuotaReserved conditions.

Read the condition message and recent events.

Compare PodSet resource requests with any admission assignments.

Follow the LocalQueue link and confirm its status and ClusterQueue.

Follow the ClusterQueue link and compare quota, reservations, usage, and policy.

Inspect ResourceFlavor or Cohort details when the message points to flavor selection or shared quota.

If admitted, move to Headlamp's Job and Pod views.

The plugin does not replace kubectl or kueuectl for scripts, exact object output, or changes. It replaces the repetitive UI-less navigation needed during an interactive investigation.

For scripts and GitOps workflows, the command line remains the right tool. For a person asking “why is this waiting?”, the linked Headlamp path is usually faster to read.

The plugin repository includes sample Cohort, ClusterQueue, LocalQueue, ResourceFlavor, and Job manifests for local testing. Apply those examples to a cluster where Kueue is installed, then open the Kueue section in Headlamp.

To reproduce the specific quota mismatch used in this article, configure a ClusterQueue with two CPUs and two Pod slots, then submit a three-Pod Job that requests one CPU per Pod. The Job enters its LocalQueue, Kueue creates a Workload, and the Workload remains pending because the combined request is larger than the available quota.

Keeping this setup separate from the main walkthrough is intentional: once the resources exist, the investigation itself should happen in Headlamp.

The 0.1.0-alpha release is an initial read-only view of the Kueue admission path.

The plugin does not add its own create, edit, pause, resume, or delete flows.

Workload owner references are displayed as text and are not currently navigable.

The UI depends on the Kubernetes permissions of the identity used by Headlamp.

Metrics and a visual relationship map are possible follow-up areas rather than features of this release.

These limits are useful to state clearly: the plugin's current purpose is to make admission state and resource relationships easier to inspect.

Compared to the 300 ms spent just setting up the connection, the HTTP request itself can be cheap to serve, taking only a little over 1 RTT when bandwidth is plentiful or the HTTP response is small.

Even in more favorable conditions, opening a new connection can still be expensive. Our opening benchmark queries index.golang.org, which is distributed worldwide and supports TLS 1.3. This means that latency is relatively low, and the TLS handshake can actually finish in just 1 RTT. Even so, the performance difference is still very noticeable.

To avoid paying this setup cost on every request, HTTP clients and servers try to reuse existing connections across multiple requests when possible. The mechanics of this differ based on the HTTP version:

In HTTP/1 (both HTTP/1.0 and HTTP/1.1), multiple requests and responses on the same connection are sent sequentially on the wire. If two GET requests are sent to index.golang.org over the same TCP connection, the responses arrive as a single stream of bytes:

As shown in the diagram, net/http.Get reads and parses the status line and headers from the byte stream to populate net/http.Response, then returns immediately. The response body remains unread on the connection and is consumed only as the caller reads from net/http.Response.Body.

Because responses are sent back-to-back on the same stream, net/http can only find the start of the next response after the current response body has been read to the very end (as determined by the Content-Length or Transfer-Encoding header).

Through Go 1.26, when resp.Body.Close is called, net/http sees that there is still an unread response body. Realizing that it cannot reuse the connection, it abandons the existing connection and creates new connections for its next requests, causing it to repeatedly incur the cost of establishing new connections.

(HTTP/2 does not have this problem. Because it multiplexes streams of frames over a single connection rather than sending responses strictly back-to-back, an unread response body on one stream does not block other requests from sharing the connection.)

This subtlety of HTTP/1 connection reuse can cause silent performance degradation in seemingly innocuous code.

As in our opening example, a client might only care about an API response’s status code. However, the API endpoint it is talking to might send bodies that provide more detailed but ultimately unused information, such as {"success": true, "message": "update processed"}. This will unfortunately cause connection reuse to fail.

Even programs that do read the response body can run into this issue if they stop just short of the end of the body. For example, encoding/json.Decoder stops reading as soon as it sees the closing } of a JSON object. If a server sends a JSON response terminated with a newline, decoding the JSON object will leave the newline still unread. The connection cannot be reused for a new request until that newline is consumed. Thus, a healthy connection might end up being abandoned, even though it was only a single byte away from being reusable.
